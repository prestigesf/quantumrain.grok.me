Quantum rain

A self-bound droplet of ultracold potassium and rubidium stretches into a filament, then breaks into a train of smaller droplets. That is the first laboratory analog of Plateau–Rayleigh instability in a quantum fluid.

What actually happened

In March 2025, Cavicchioli, Fort, and collaborators at LENS / CNR-INO / Florence reported the dynamical formation of multiple quantum droplets in a heteronuclear Bose–Bose mixture. They quenched a ⁴¹K–⁸⁷Rb gas from non-interacting to strongly attractive inside an optical waveguide. A single self-bound droplet formed in an excited compression-elongation mode, stretched past a critical length, and split into two or more smaller droplets.

The number of fragments rose as interspecies attraction decreased and atom number increased — the signature of capillary (Plateau–Rayleigh) instability driven by a quantum surface tension. Mean-field attraction tries to collapse the cloud; Lee–Huang–Yang fluctuations stabilize a liquid-like droplet that can then rain.

A stretching quantum droplet breaks like water leaving a faucet.

Cavicchioli et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 134, 093401 (7 March 2025)

Why the pieces matter

Self-bound droplets

Stabilized by a contest between attractive mean-field forces and repulsive LHY fluctuations. No box required.

Capillary analog

The same wavelength selection that breaks a water jet appears here. Most-unstable mode near λ ≈ 9R₀.

Ordered arrays

Controlled fragmentation is a path to droplet lattices — addressable mesoscopic quantum objects.

Hydrodynamics testbed

A clean place to watch nonlinear quantum fluid dynamics in a two-component bosonic mixture.

How to read the canvas

The simulation is a visual model, not a Gross–Pitaevskii integrator. Particles cohere into a filament, feel a surface-tension-like force, and pick up fluctuation noise. Raise stretch or hit Trigger breakup to cross the instability. Attraction tightens the droplet; fluctuations seed the pinches the way LHY jitter seeds a real filament.
